# How company type changes the answer

**Two axes, and they are independent.** What you sell, and how it gets bought. A B2B SaaS product
can be PLG or SLG; a service is almost never PLG. Treating them as one axis is the error that makes
most advice untransferable.

---

## Axis 1: what you sell

| | **SaaS** | **Service** | **Physical product** |
|---|---|---|---|
| What the buyer risks | Switching cost, data, adoption | Trusting specific people | Capital, downtime, safety |
| What proof looks like | Trial, usage, integrations | Named people, prior work, process | Deployments, specs, certifications |
| Who blocks the deal | IT, security, procurement | Nobody formally; trust fails quietly | Engineering, safety, finance |
| How fast to value | Days to weeks | Weeks | Months, often after commissioning |
| What content must do | Reduce perceived switching cost | Make specific humans credible | Reduce the risk of an irreversible spend |

**The under-appreciated one is services.** A service sale is a bet on people, so **the content that
works is the content with a person's name on it.** This is why founder-led content is
disproportionately effective for agencies and consultancies and only moderately effective for
enterprise software. The buyer is literally evaluating whether they want this person in their
business.

**Physical products carry a constraint software does not: claims are checkable and wrong ones are
expensive.** A capability claim about a physical product can be tested on site, and a certification claim is
a regulatory exposure rather than a marketing overreach. This is why the Gate exists as a hard
binary in the engine rather than as a style guideline.

---

## Axis 2: how it gets bought

| | **PLG** | **SLG** | **PLS** | **Enterprise or capex** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Who decides | One user | A committee | A user, then a committee | A committee with a budget cycle |
| Content's job | Get to value alone | Shortlist, then arm a champion | Make the expansion conversation welcome | Survive procurement and engineering review |
| Winning format | Product-led education, docs, templates | Proof depth, objection handling, comparisons | Usage-triggered relevance | Specificity, references, risk reduction |
| Cycle | Days | Months | Months after weeks | Quarters, tied to budget |
| Where content is consumed | Inside the product | In a shortlist conversation you never see | Both | Forwarded internally as a PDF |

**The single most useful distinction: in PLG the reader is the buyer; in SLG the reader is often a
messenger.** SLG content has a second audience it never meets, which is the rest of the committee.
That changes the artefact: **it has to survive being forwarded without you in the room.** A case
study that only makes sense with a salesperson explaining it has failed at its actual job.

**Most published content advice is written for PLG SaaS.** It reads plausible everywhere because the
vocabulary is generic, and it transfers badly because the buying model underneath is different. This
is the most common reason a content strategy underperforms with nobody able to say why.

---

## The combinations that behave differently from the sum of their parts

**Service plus SLG.** Founder and executive content is not one tactic among many, it is the primary
channel, because the buyer is evaluating people. The company page is nearly decorative.

**Physical product plus capex.** The buying committee contains at least one person whose job is to
find the reason not to proceed. **Content that never addresses the blocker loses deals invisibly**,
because the blocker's objection is raised in a room you are not in. Safety, integration risk and
maintenance burden are content topics, not just sales objections.

**SaaS plus PLS.** The content has to work twice for the same person: once to get them using it, and
later to make the commercial conversation feel like a natural next step rather than a bait and
switch.
