# Objective elicitation

**The method for finding out what a company's content is actually for**, and turning that into
weights the engine can use.

*(Ruling 3, 11 Sep 2026: the objective "needs to be catered to specifically for every industry and
company". So this is a method, not a form.)*

---

## Why this needs a method at all

Ask a company what content is for and you get: leads, brand, thought leadership, recruiting, SEO,
and investor optics. All of it, equally, sincerely. **Nobody is lying.** They are answering a
question about *value* when the engine needs an answer about *tradeoff*.

Three specific failures follow, and the method exists to defeat each one.

**Everything is important.** Weights that come out near-equal produce a scorer that cannot rank
anything, which is the same as having no scorer.

**Stated and revealed objectives differ.** What a company says it wants and what it funds, approves
and protects are routinely different things. The gap is not dishonesty; it is that nobody has
reconciled the two. **The gap is itself one of the most useful findings the intake produces.**

**Objectives have different clocks.** "Pipeline this quarter" and "be a known name in three years"
are not comparable quantities, and weighting them against each other without saying so compares a
sprint to a pension.

---

## Before the session: revealed preference

**Gather this first. It is the control against which stated answers are checked.**

| What to collect | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| Last four quarters of marketing spend by line | What actually got funded |
| The last three things that were approved fast | What the company treats as urgent |
| The last three things that were killed or deferred | What it treats as optional |
| Who was hired into marketing, and for what | The bet being made |
| What gets reported to the board or investors | What leadership is measured on |
| What the sales team asks marketing for, repeatedly | The unserved need |

**Do not show this to the person before the session.** It is the comparison, and showing it changes
the answer you are testing.

---

## The session, eight steps

Roughly 40 minutes with one person. Run it separately with each stakeholder rather than as a group,
for reasons in the multi-stakeholder section.

### Step 1: generate candidates

Offer a menu and invite additions. The menu exists because unprompted answers cluster on whatever
the person was last asked about.

> Pipeline and qualified conversations · Category presence and reputation · Supporting deals already
> in progress · Recruiting · Investor and partner confidence · Customer retention and expansion ·
> Defending against a specific competitor · Educating a market that does not know the category exists

**Cap at six.** More than six and the exercise stops discriminating.

### Step 2: separate objectives from constraints

**This is the step most often skipped and it silently corrupts the weights.**

An objective is something you want *more* of. A constraint is a line you must not cross. "Do not
damage the brand", "never overstate a capability", "do not upset the channel partners" are
constraints. **They are floors, not goals, and they belong in the Gate rather than the weighting.**

Ask of each candidate: *would more of this always be better?* If no, it is a constraint. Move it.

### Step 3: assign a clock to each

For each surviving objective: **when would you expect to see it working?** One quarter, one year,
three years.

**Objectives on different clocks cannot share one budget honestly.** Two practical options, and the
company picks:

- **Split the budget by horizon.** For example 70 points across near-term objectives and 30 across
  long-term, then allocate within each. Keeps a slow objective from being crushed by an urgent one.
- **One budget, horizon recorded.** Simpler, and acceptable when the clocks are similar.

**The failure this prevents:** a long-horizon objective always loses a naive allocation, because
this quarter's pressure is vivid and three years away is abstract. Then the company wonders in two
years why it never became known for anything.

### Step 4: allocate 100 points

Constant sum, not ranking. **Ranking loses intensity**: it cannot tell 60/40 from 95/5, and those
are completely different instructions to a content engine.

> You have 100 points. Distribute them across these objectives to reflect how you would want a
> hundred pieces of content allocated. It must total 100.

**Two rules on the output:**

- **Nothing below 10 survives.** An objective at 5 points will never change a decision and is noise
  in the model. Cut it or merge it.
- **If the top objective is under 40, the company has not chosen.** Go to step 5 and come back.

### Step 5: the sacrifice test

**The question that does the real work.**

> To get more of your top objective, what are you willing to accept less of?

Then, pointed:

> If content produced **zero** of [second objective] for two quarters, but doubled [top objective],
> would you take that trade?

A yes confirms the ranking. A no means the second objective is worth more than the allocation says,
and the points are wrong. **Re-allocate, do not argue.**

### Step 6: the regret test

Prospective hindsight. It surfaces what the direct question misses, because people forecast loss
more accurately than gain.

> It is twelve months from now. You got everything you asked for on your top objective and nothing
> at all on the others. Is that a good year or a bad year?

> Same date. You got nothing on your top objective but a great deal of [second]. How do you explain
> that to your board?

**Discomfort in the second answer is the signal.** If they can explain it comfortably, the second
objective is doing more work than they admitted.

### Step 7: pairwise consistency

For three or more objectives, check for a loop. Ask which of each pair matters more, and look for
A over B, B over C, C over A.

**A loop is not a mistake to be corrected on the spot.** It usually means the objectives are being
compared on different dimensions, or that two of them are the same thing under different names.
**Name what you found and go back to step 1.** A quietly resolved loop produces weights nobody
believes later.

### Step 8: conflict or alignment

For each pair, ask: **does one piece of content serve both, or must you choose?**

- **Aligned pair.** The same content serves both. The weighting matters less, and the engine can
  optimise for one and get the other for free.
- **Conflicting pair.** Serving one costs the other. **The weight is load-bearing and every
  recommendation will turn on it.**

**Name the axis of conflict**, because it is rarely the topic. Most often it is **audience**
(different readers), **register** (what tone each requires), or **time** (one pays back this
quarter, the other next year).

*Worked example.* A company states two objectives: qualified conversations with buyers, and being
known in its sector. **Partly aligned, and the conflict is audience, not topic.** Content that
makes it known to the people who buy serves both. Content that makes it known to the wider
industry, at conferences and among peers, serves only the second. So the split is less "which
objective" and more **"which room"**, and that is what the weights actually encode.

## Multi-stakeholder, and why separately

**Run this with each stakeholder alone**, at minimum whoever owns revenue, whoever owns marketing,
and the founder or CEO.

In a group the most senior answer becomes everyone's answer within about ninety seconds, and you
have learned one opinion at the cost of three sessions.

**Disagreement is the finding, not a problem to smooth over.** Three patterns and what each means:

| Pattern | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Sales wants deal support, marketing wants category presence | Normal and healthy tension | Weight it deliberately and say so |
| Founder's answer differs from everyone else's | The stated strategy has not been transmitted | Escalate. Content cannot fix this |
| Everyone agrees instantly and completely | Usually means nobody has thought about it | Run the sacrifice test harder |

**Record each person's allocation separately in the profile**, then record the agreed one and who
agreed it. In six months, "the CBO said this on 11 Sep" is auditable; "the objective is X" is not.

---

## Reconciling stated against revealed

Compare the allocation from the session against what the pre-work showed.

**A gap of more than about 30 points on any objective is worth stopping for.** The usual causes, in
order of frequency:

1. **The stated objective is aspirational**, and spend reflects the real one. The engine should use
   the revealed weight, or the recommendations will be ignored.
2. **The strategy genuinely changed recently** and spend has not caught up. Use the stated weight,
   and note the date.
3. **Marketing and leadership want different things.** Not a weighting problem. Escalate it.

**Write the gap into the profile either way.** A profile recording that the company says presence
and funds pipeline is more useful than one that has quietly picked a side.

## What goes into the profile

```yaml
objectives:
  elicited_on: 2026-09-11
  method: eight-step, run separately with 3 stakeholders
  budget_split: { near_term: 70, long_term: 30 }   # if horizons differ
  agreed:
    - name: qualified plant conversations
      weight: 60
      horizon: 1-2 quarters
      measured_by: inbound conversations with plant or operations heads
    - name: known in the sector
      weight: 40
      horizon: 2-3 years
      measured_by: [OPEN] needs an operational definition
  constraints:                    # from step 2, these are Gate items
    - no unverifiable capability claims
    - worker-positive framing always
  conflicts:
    - pair: [qualified plant conversations, known in the sector]
      type: partial
      axis: audience          # the people who buy vs the wider industry
  stated_vs_revealed:
    gap: [TO BE FILLED after the pre-work]
  per_stakeholder:
    - who: CBO
      allocation: {...}
  agreed_by: [names]
  review_on: 2026-12-11
```

**Two fields carry unusual weight.**

`measured_by` **must be a thing that can be observed**, or the objective cannot be validated and the
engine is scoring against a wish. If it cannot be filled, say so in the open (as above) rather than
inventing a proxy.

`review_on` **exists because objectives expire.** A stale objective is worse than none, because the
engine keeps optimising confidently for something the company stopped caring about. Default six
months, or immediately after a funding round, a pivot, or a change of marketing leadership.

## Failure modes

**Weights that sum to 100 and mean nothing.** Symptom: three objectives at 34/33/33. Cause: steps 5
and 6 were skipped. The allocation is arithmetic, not a decision.

**Constraints weighted as objectives.** Symptom: "brand safety" at 20 points. Effect: the engine
trades safety against reach, which is not a trade anyone intends.

**The founder was not asked.** Symptom: the weights get overruled in month two by someone who was
never in the room.

**A long-horizon objective with no `measured_by`.** Symptom: "be known in the sector" with nothing
observable behind it. It then silently absorbs whatever the other objectives cannot explain, and
becomes unfalsifiable.

**Eliciting once and never again.** Objectives are the most perishable field in the profile.

## When the instrument fails rather than the company

**Three faults, each found by running this on two executives and getting no weights from either.**
All three produce an answer that looks like data and is not.

**Page-one truncation.** A five-page pack came back with pages two onward blank, both times. The
allocation is the question that produces weights and it was not on page one. **An instrument that
reliably returns three answers beats one that asks for eight and returns none.** Cut everything
past the sacrifice test; the regret and conflict questions are good and were never reached.

**A menu with an obvious answer.** Two people, asked separately, returned identical lists. That
reads as consensus and was not: two of the seven options were plainly work that channel does not
do, so both removed exactly those two. **That tests reading comprehension, not preference.** Show
a channel only the objectives it can plausibly serve, so every option on the page is a real
choice. And ask for the allocation directly: a keep-or-drop menu invites keeping most of them,
and five of seven kept unranked narrows nothing.

**Scope recorded nowhere.** One person was asked to allocate across all content; the others were
asked about their own channel. Those are different questions, so the answers cannot be averaged,
ranked against each other, or reconciled. They sat in one list looking like disagreement.
**Record the scope on every allocation**, and never compare across two.

---

## Before you run this at all, ask what the channel is made of

**The hardest lesson here, and it arrived last.** An executive channel whose calendar is sourced
from what those executives have already published does not need their stated objectives. Their
back catalogue *is* revealed preference, and this document's own pre-work section says to gather
that first and treat it as the control.

Two sessions produced no weights, and the correct answer had been sitting in the repository the
whole time in the form of nine published articles.

**So: before eliciting objectives for a channel, ask what that channel will be made out of. If a
body of work already exists, read that instead.** Stated objectives earn their place where there
is no track record to read. Where there is one, they are the weaker source and cost somebody's
time to get.

## The failure that gets added at ranking time

**Step 3 warns that a long-horizon objective loses a naive allocation. It loses again at ranking,
after a perfectly fair one.**

Two objectives on a one-to-two-quarter clock and a two-to-three-year clock, weighted 67 and 33. A
single blended score puts almost every long-horizon item below almost every short-horizon one,
because two thirds of the weight sits on the near clock. Nothing is wrong with the weights. The
ranking is comparing a sprint against a pension, one item at a time.

**Rank inside a clock, never across one.** `engine/decision/scoring.py` returns `fit.by_kind`
alongside the blended score for exactly this reason.

**Add to step 3:** if the horizons differ, say so in the profile *and* check that whatever
consumes the weights can rank within a horizon. **A budget split honoured at allocation time and
discarded at ranking time is not a budget split.**
